Eli Young

I research how to teach and assess programming with AI.

I graduated from Swarthmore College in 2026 with a B.A. in computer science. I work in human-computer interaction and computing education with Michael Wehar and Andy Luu, and I co-founded AlgoArena, a coding platform for practice, classes, and hiring.

I also did machine learning research with Gabriel Hope at Swarthmore and in Mohammed Kamruzzaman’s lab at the University of Illinois.

Research

Many CS classes either forbid AI or pretend it doesn’t exist, and test suites check the final code without showing how it was written or whether its author understood it. My research asks how to teach students to build with AI, whether skill with AI can be measured from the way a person works, and whether a model can learn to do that scoring reliably.

Assessment. We proposed a framework that scores a recorded programming session on five competencies: how the student breaks down the problem, plans, prompts, checks the model’s output, and steers an agent that goes wrong. It is a proposal, not yet a validated instrument. To make validation possible, I built much of AlgoArena’s assessment environment, an editor where the model can change files and run commands, and where every prompt, edit, test run, and diff review is saved as a replayable trace.

Teaching. We wrote an open curriculum for building software by directing a model: writing a specification, supervising coding agents, and checking what they produce. We are expanding it into a free textbook, Vibecoding: Introduction to Applied AI.

Practice in class. AlgoArena Classroom runs live quizzes in CS courses. Students predict a program’s output, give an algorithm’s running time, write code that is graded against test cases, or draw a data structure. Michael Wehar piloted it in his Competitive Coding Algorithms course (CS 283) at Bryn Mawr College in spring 2026. From June to November 2026, four outside hosts ran eight live quizzes with three or more participants, the largest with seventeen. The pilot was too small to show an effect on learning, so a full course evaluation comes next.

From here I want to work with faculty to try this teaching in courses they already teach, compare human graders with automated scoring, and train models to score past sessions reliably.

Before this I worked on applied machine learning. As an NSF REU fellow in Mohammed Kamruzzaman’s lab at the University of Illinois, mentored by Marcus da Silva Ferreira, I predicted egg viability and chick sex from daily hyperspectral images of incubating eggs, reaching 93.8% viability accuracy by day four with all model fitting kept inside the validation folds. I’m writing the manuscript as first author. At Swarthmore, advised by Gabriel Hope, I classified insect feeding behavior from electrical recordings for a USDA ARS project.

Projects

AlgoArena

Co-founder and CEO · 2025 to present

Next.js · TypeScript · Firebase · AWS Bedrock

A coding platform for practice, classes, and hiring. It runs live 1v1 matches, the classroom quizzes and AI coding assessment described above, and more than 9,100 problems judged in 17 languages. About 1,100 people have registered accounts, Bryn Mawr College paid for the pilot subscription through a Digital Bryn Mawr grant, and we ran an AI coding hackathon at the University of Rhode Island.

I write much of the code, usually by running several coding agents at once.

Lock In Squad

Founder and sole engineer · 2024 to present

React Native · Swift · Kotlin · Firebase

A free app blocker for iPhone and Android. You pick the apps to block and start a focus session, alone or with friends, and ending a session early takes a deliberate hold. About 1,100 accounts.

The blocking itself runs in native Swift and Kotlin modules.

Game Combiner

Solo project · 2026

Python · FastAPI · sentence-transformers · Cloud Run

Drag two video games together and get a third. Every game in a catalog of about 140,000 from Steam and IGDB has an embedding, and the site searches near the midpoint of the two you combine, with scoring rules that give every player the same result for the same pair. Whoever finds a combination first gets credit for it.
